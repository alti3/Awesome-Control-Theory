## What It Means

**Full-state feedback** is a Feedback & Feedforward topic in the **Core Feedback Concepts** branch of the control-theory map. In this context, it focuses on the part of the control workflow described by this map category. It belongs to the broader control-theory workflow, so the important question is not only what the method computes, but what assumptions make the computation meaningful for a closed loop.

A useful way to read this topic is as a connection between three objects: a model of the plant or signal, an objective stated in engineering terms, and evidence that the resulting behavior is stable, accurate, robust, or safe enough for the application.

## Mathematical Form

For a linear state-space model, full-state feedback is commonly written

$$
\dot x=Ax+Bu,\qquad u=-Kx+k_r r.
$$

The gain $K$ changes the closed-loop matrix to $A-BK$. The reference gain $k_r$
or an integral augmentation is added when the problem is tracking rather than
pure regulation.

## Intuition

The key intuition is to ask what information this topic adds to the feedback loop. Some topics add a model, some add a controller, some add an estimator, and some add a test. For **Full-state feedback**, the central issue is the behavior summarized above: uses the entire state vector, often as u = -Kx, when states are measured or estimated. The method still has to remain consistent with the plant assumptions and the performance objective.

A practical example is an engineering design review: the topic helps decide what model is trusted, what signals are measured, what behavior is required, and what evidence is needed before implementation.

Full-state feedback naturally follows reachability because pole assignment and LQR
only work in the controllable part of the state. In examples such as vehicle
steering, population regulation, drug administration, and vectored-thrust aircraft,
the state vector gives a compact way to coordinate variables that would be awkward
to tune as independent SISO loops.

## How It Is Used

1. Specify the signals, units, sampling convention, operating point, and uncertainty assumptions.
2. Choose the representation that matches the topic: transfer function, state-space model, graph, stochastic model, optimization problem, or certificate.
3. Apply the method to obtain a controller, estimate, trajectory, reduced model, stability statement, or diagnostic quantity.
4. Check the result against the original engineering requirement, including actuator limits, disturbances, noise, and implementation constraints.
5. Validate with an independent calculation, simulation, experiment, or residual check before relying on the conclusion.

## Common Pitfalls

- Check that the linear model is valid over the amplitudes and operating points used in the design.
- Define assumptions explicitly: time domain, sampling, uncertainty, measured outputs, actuator limits, and disturbances.
