## What It Means

**PID tuning** chooses $K_p$, $K_i$, and $K_d$ so a PID loop meets stability,
tracking, disturbance rejection, noise, and actuator-effort requirements. Tuning is
a design problem, not just a recipe: rules such as Ziegler-Nichols are starting
points that must be checked against margins and implementation limits.

## Mathematical Form

A filtered PID controller is often written

$$
C(s)=K_p+\frac{K_i}{s}+\frac{K_d s}{1+sT_f}.
$$

The derivative filter $T_f$ matters because ideal differentiation is not physically
realizable and amplifies measurement noise.

## Intuition

Proportional action sets immediate correction, integral action removes steady
error, and derivative action adds prediction-like damping. Tuning balances those
effects against noise, delay, and saturation.

## How It Is Used

1. Start from a plant model, step test, relay test, or frequency response.
2. Choose gains for a desired bandwidth and damping.
3. Check gain margin, phase margin, sensitivity peak, actuator effort, and noise.
4. Add anti-windup and derivative filtering before implementation.

## Common Pitfalls

- Using aggressive tuning rules without checking robustness.
- Leaving derivative action unfiltered.
- Forgetting that integral action can wind up during saturation or mode changes.
