## What It Means

**Positive feedback** feeds a signal back with a sign that reinforces the current
deviation instead of reducing it. In linear models this can easily destabilize the
loop, but in real systems it is often paired with saturation, hysteresis, switching,
or other nonlinear effects to create useful behavior.

Positive feedback appears in amplifiers, oscillators, biological activation,
integral-action realizations, and switch-like behavior. The point is not that
positive feedback is "bad"; it is that the designer must account for the nonlinear
mechanism that limits growth.

## Mathematical Form

For a scalar static loop,

$$
y = P(u + k y).
$$

With linear $P$, increasing $k$ can move the closed-loop denominator toward zero.
With nonlinear $P$, saturation or a sector bound can create multiple equilibria,
limit cycles, or bistable behavior.

## Intuition

Negative feedback suppresses error; positive feedback amplifies a tendency that is
already present. That makes it useful when the goal is decisive switching,
memory-like behavior, or oscillation, and risky when the plant can run away.

## How It Is Used

1. Identify the variable being reinforced and the physical limit that prevents
   unbounded growth.
2. Analyze equilibria and local stability, not just small-signal loop gain.
3. Check for saturation, hysteresis, dead zones, or logic that changes the
   effective loop.
4. Simulate large-signal behavior from several initial conditions.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating a positive-feedback design as a purely linear small-signal problem.
- Ignoring actuator or amplifier saturation that actually determines the final
  operating point.
- Confusing useful regenerative behavior with robust regulation.
