## What It Means

**Aerospace & pointing models** is an foundational tools topic in the **First Principles & Classical Tools** branch of the control-theory map. In this context, it focuses on the part of the control workflow described by this map category. It belongs to physical and classical modeling tools that connect engineering assumptions to mathematical control models, so the important question is not only what the method computes, but what assumptions make the computation meaningful for a closed loop.

A useful way to read this topic is as a connection between three objects: a model of the plant or signal, an objective stated in engineering terms, and evidence that the resulting behavior is stable, accurate, robust, or safe enough for the application.

## Mathematical Form

A useful control-theory abstraction is a dynamical map with an objective:

$$
\dot x=f(x,u,d,t),\qquad y=h(x,u,t),\qquad e=r-y.
$$

The notation should be interpreted with the page topic in mind: $x$ is the state or configuration, $u$ is the manipulated input, $y$ is the measured output, $r$ is a reference, $e$ is an error signal, and disturbances or modeling errors are included when they materially affect the design.

## Intuition

The key intuition is to ask what information this topic adds to the feedback loop. Some topics add a model, some add a controller, some add an estimator, and some add a test. For **Aerospace & pointing models**, the central issue is the behavior summarized above: satellite attitude, antenna azimuth, aircraft landing, and servomotor dynamics. The method still has to remain consistent with the plant assumptions and the performance objective.

A practical example is an engineering design review: the topic helps decide what model is trusted, what signals are measured, what behavior is required, and what evidence is needed before implementation.

Flight-control examples show how modeling, linearization, state feedback,
estimation, and loop shaping fit together. A vectored-thrust VTOL aircraft is
especially useful: the same thrust geometry couples roll angle, lateral
acceleration, and vertical motion, so the model naturally leads to nested inner
attitude and outer position loops. Rockets use the same idea in a different
geometry when a gimbaled nozzle creates the stabilizing moment.

## Thrust Vector Control

Thrust vector control belongs here because it is a first-principles aerospace
model before it is a controller. The designer starts with force and moment balance,
maps actuator geometry into forces and torques, then decides which variables are
measured or estimated. Near hover or a trim condition, the nonlinear rigid-body
model is linearized so LQR, observers, Kalman filters, or loop-shaping methods can
be applied.

The architecture matters: a fast inner loop regulates attitude or angular rate,
while a slower outer loop commands position, velocity, or acceleration. If the
outer loop asks for more attitude or thrust than the vehicle can provide, the
linear design no longer represents the closed loop.

## How It Is Used

1. Specify the signals, units, sampling convention, operating point, and uncertainty assumptions.
2. Choose the representation that matches the topic: transfer function, state-space model, graph, stochastic model, optimization problem, or certificate.
3. Apply the method to obtain a controller, estimate, trajectory, reduced model, stability statement, or diagnostic quantity.
4. Check the result against the original engineering requirement, including actuator limits, disturbances, noise, and implementation constraints.
5. Validate with an independent calculation, simulation, experiment, or residual check before relying on the conclusion.

## Common Pitfalls

- Define assumptions explicitly: time domain, sampling, uncertainty, measured outputs, actuator limits, and disturbances.
- Verify conclusions with simulation or analysis that matches the intended closed-loop interconnection.
- Check trim conditions, actuator authority, and rate limits before trusting a
  hover or small-angle linearization.
