## What It Means

**Top-down architectures** begin with mission or system-level requirements and
decompose them into layers: planning, discrete logic, reference generation,
estimation, and low-level feedback. Autonomous systems and process control are
common examples where high-level decisions must be translated into safe, trackable
commands.

## Mathematical Form

A top-down stack can be summarized as

$$
\text{mission} \rightarrow \text{plan} \rightarrow r(t) \rightarrow u(t),
$$

with constraints and state estimates flowing upward.

## Intuition

The top layer decides what should happen; lower layers make it dynamically
feasible. The architecture is successful when high-level decisions respect what the
plant and low-level loops can actually do.

## How It Is Used

1. Define objectives, constraints, and safety requirements first.
2. Decompose them into planning, logic, estimation, and feedback responsibilities.
3. Specify interfaces between layers.
4. Verify that lower layers can track references generated above them.

## Common Pitfalls

- Generating references that violate plant dynamics or actuator limits.
- Treating safety logic as an afterthought.
- Leaving layer interfaces informal.
