## What It Means

**LQR** is a linear topic in the **Control Methods** branch of the control-theory map. In this context, it focuses on linear feedback design, superposition, pole locations, loop shaping, and implementable compensators. It belongs to controller design methods that turn models, measurements, and objectives into feedback or feedforward actions, so the important question is not only what the method computes, but what assumptions make the computation meaningful for a closed loop.

A useful way to read this topic is as a connection between three objects: a model of the plant or signal, an objective stated in engineering terms, and evidence that the resulting behavior is stable, accurate, robust, or safe enough for the application.

## Mathematical Form

For linear-quadratic regulation, the feedback gain follows from a Riccati solution:

$$
u=-Kx,\qquad K=R^{-1}B^T P.
$$

The notation should be interpreted with the page topic in mind: $x$ is the state or configuration, $u$ is the manipulated input, $y$ is the measured output, $r$ is a reference, $e$ is an error signal, and disturbances or modeling errors are included when they materially affect the design.

## Intuition

The key intuition is to ask what information this topic adds to the feedback loop. Some topics add a model, some add a controller, some add an estimator, and some add a test. For **LQR**, the central issue is the behavior summarized above: linear Quadratic Regulator minimizes a quadratic state and input cost for optimal state feedback. The method still has to remain consistent with the plant assumptions and the performance objective.

A practical example is an engineering design review: the topic helps decide what model is trusted, what signals are measured, what behavior is required, and what evidence is needed before implementation.

LQR bridges reachability, state feedback, and practical design tradeoffs. In the
vectored-thrust aircraft example, the state penalty encodes how much lateral
position, velocity, attitude, and angular rate matter, while the input penalty
encodes limited thrust authority. The Riccati solution turns those engineering
weights into a stabilizing gain when the linearized pair is reachable.

## Weight Selection

The common workflow is to scale each state and input by an acceptable excursion
and then tune from the resulting behavior. Bryson-style scaling is not a proof of
optimality for the real plant; it is a disciplined way to avoid arbitrary units
dominating the cost.

## How It Is Used

1. Specify the signals, units, sampling convention, operating point, and uncertainty assumptions.
2. Choose the representation that matches the topic: transfer function, state-space model, graph, stochastic model, optimization problem, or certificate.
3. Apply the method to obtain a controller, estimate, trajectory, reduced model, stability statement, or diagnostic quantity.
4. Check the result against the original engineering requirement, including actuator limits, disturbances, noise, and implementation constraints.
5. Validate with an independent calculation, simulation, experiment, or residual check before relying on the conclusion.

## Common Pitfalls

- Check that the linear model is valid over the amplitudes and operating points used in the design.
- Define assumptions explicitly: time domain, sampling, uncertainty, measured outputs, actuator limits, and disturbances.
- Remember that LQR handles unconstrained linear-quadratic regulation; saturation
  and rate limits still require separate checks.
